The challenge closed forty-four days before I read the rules.

Not after I built something. Before I opened the editor. I had the idea, the stack, and a five-phase plan on the table, and the first line of the contest page said the entry period ended on October 5. It was November 18.

## The calendar is part of the brief

The challenge was "Hacktoberfest Weekend: Build for a Friend". Entries ran from October 2 to October 5, 2026. Winners were announced on October 9. By the time we sat down, the prize had an owner, the repository window had closed, and the only honest verdict was "not eligible".

That is not a detail you discover in review. It is the first constraint, and it was the one nobody checked. A challenge is a calendar with a theme attached. The theme is the part everyone reads. The calendar is the part that decides whether there is a submission at all.

## The second gate

Even with the window open, the post would not have competed. The rules said that non-English submissions were eligible for a completion badge, not for prizes. The draft was in Spanish, written for a local audience. The audience was right and the submission was wrong.

Two gates, both outside the code. Neither one was in the plan. We had spent the thinking on models, embeddings and a local RAG stack, and none of that mattered until those two gates were open.

## The plan was a table of contents

The audit scored the plan 9 out of 25. Not because the idea was bad. Because the plan had not made a decision.

"Choose a friend with a private information problem" is a segment, not a person. "A local RAG agent" is the most repeated demo of the last two years. "A lightweight framework" is a placeholder. "Docker or a script" is a fork in the road left open. There were five phases, three data modalities, no cut lines, and one paragraph for writing quality, the criterion weighted most heavily.

The scope was three to four weeks of work in a 72-hour window. PDFs, voice notes, CSVs, embeddings, an agent, verification, Docker, a UI and a handover. Without cut lines, the predictable outcome is that 80 percent is half-built and the post is written at the end, tired, from memory.

A plan that does not choose cannot be executed. It can be improvised, which is a different activity.

## What the audit changed

It named the person: Lucía, a freelance photographer with a decade of contracts and invoices she cannot upload. It named one task, one action, one twist: the button that deletes everything, with proof. It put the post in English and gave it 30 to 40 percent of the time. It cut voice notes, CSV and Docker before cutting citations, evaluation, or the friend's reaction.

It also read the judging criteria as a product brief. There were 17 winners across an overall prize, six featured categories and ten partner categories. The original plan had not chosen a category, which is leaving value on the table before the first commit. The rules also said the project had to be new during the entry period, so a repo started earlier would not qualify no matter how good it was.

The most useful part was not the list of fixes. It was the order: eligibility first, then the person, then the one action, then the cut lines, then the stack. The stack is the last decision, not the first.

## The writing is the deliverable

The challenge weighted Writing Quality most heavily. The original plan treated the post as documentation to write after the build. That is backwards for a challenge where judges read the post and may never run the repository.

The corrected plan starts the post on the first day, captures screenshots while the thing is being built, and keeps two hours of buffer before the deadline. The post is not a report of the work. It is the work in the form the judge receives it.

That changes what you build. A demo that cannot be explained in one screenshot is a demo that will not score. A twist that needs three paragraphs of setup is not a twist. Writing the post first forces the product to have a shape.

## The rule

Read the rules before the roadmap. Check the calendar, the language, the eligibility, and the scoring weights. A challenge is not a prompt; it is a contract with a deadline.

Then make one decision per phase. Who is the person? What is the single action? What is the twist someone will remember? What gets cut when the clock runs out? If a plan cannot answer those, it is not a plan yet.

We did not build for that challenge. We built the audit that stopped us from wasting a weekend, and the corrected plan is still on the shelf for the next window. The cheapest lesson is the one you learn before writing code.
